# R1: The writing craft of Arcane and comparable prestige animated series

Research dossier for Death Ride narrative design. Written 2026-10-04.

**Scope.** This dossier asks how the writing in a small set of prestige animated works actually functions, and what that means for a top-down vehicular combat racer with a debt-and-defection campaign. It extracts craft only. No plot, character or line from these works should appear in Death Ride.

**Works chosen, and why.** I picked them for evidence, not fame: each has public statements from its writers or showrunners about method, and each exercises a pattern Death Ride needs. They are Arcane (humanised crime boss, class geography, a fixed ending), Avatar: The Last Airbender (the slow defection arc), Samurai Jack season 5 and Primal (turning by witness, wordless storytelling), Cowboy Bebop (debt, a past that comes to collect, a crew that is not quite a family), Invincible (a planted betrayal), Castlevania (grief-driven villain, dialogue as trade, an exploited craftsman), Into the Spider-Verse (a family-driven villain, an uncle who refuses the kill), Blue Eye Samurai (patience) and Love, Death + Robots (short-form economy, which matters for three-line cards).

**Evidence quality.** Several primary interviews (IndieWire, Variety, Deadline, Hollywood Reporter) sit behind paywalls and could not be read in full. Where a claim rests on a secondary summary, fan wiki or analysis essay, the confidence note says so. Confidence levels mean:

- **High:** the writer's own words, read directly, or a scene that can be checked on screen.
- **Medium:** a reputable secondary report, or a quote seen only in a search excerpt.
- **Low:** fan analysis, or something I inferred myself.

---

## Part 1. Patterns

Each pattern is stated as a rule, followed by the naive version that fails, a concrete example, its source, and a confidence level.

### P1. Humanise the villain through one protected relationship, then make the climax charge for it

**Rule.** When an antagonist must be both frightening and mourned, give them exactly one person they would trade the war for. Show that bond early in small, wordless gestures, and build the climax so that keeping the bond costs them the goal. We then judge the villain by what they choose, not by a backstory we were told.

**Naive failure.** A sad-backstory monologue placed just before the boss fight. The audience is asked to pity the villain instead of watching them pay for something. Pity that arrives without a cost reads as manipulation.

**Example.** In Arcane, Silco runs drugs and murders people. Across season 1 he is shown to prefer his adopted daughter Jinx to his ambitions: he protects her from his lieutenants' criticism and keeps her close even as she grows dangerous. In the season 1 finale she shoots him by accident, and he spends his last breath reassuring her rather than blaming her. It works because the season had already shown him choose her over his plans. Alex Yee, who wrote most of Silco's voice, says the character was invented from scratch, and that "his power is in the way he influences others and the relationship he has with them." Castlevania's Dracula turns genocidal after his wife's execution. Warren Ellis: "every person in the show needed to be an actual, functioning person. That had to go double for Dracula." Spider-Verse's Kingpin builds his machine to bring back the wife and son he lost.

**Sources.** Yee (AwardsDaily, SyFy Wire); Ellis (Bleeding Cool); film text.

**Confidence.** High.

### P2. Give the villain a legitimate grievance and an illegitimate method

**Rule.** When the conflict must feel two-sided, let the antagonist be right about the problem and wrong about the solution. The hero should be right about the method and complicit in the problem. Grayness then comes from the structure, not from a mood of vague ambiguity.

**Naive failure.** One of two:

- A strawman villain whose goal is evil for its own sake.
- Both-sidesing so even that nothing is at stake and no choice matters.

**Example.** In Arcane, Silco wants independence for the undercity, and the show supports the grievance visually: the upper city is bright and ordered, and the lower city is dense and polluted. His method, though, is a drug economy and terror. Vander, the hero figure, keeps the peace through a private truce with the upper city's enforcers. That truce is decent and also complicit. Christian Linke says the team "needed to take certain liberties to really have Piltover and Zaun support our story," meaning the setting was reshaped to carry the conflict. Aaron Ehasz, head writer of Avatar, describes his preferred design as "a less clear pure bad guy from the start. The sides are a bit more complicated and gray."

**Sources.** Linke (ONE Esports interview); Ehasz (korranews interview, reposted on Tumblr); Fantasy/Animation essay on Arcane's class geography (a secondary analysis).

**Confidence.** High for the quotes. Medium for the class-geography reading, which is a critic's essay, though it can be checked on screen.

### P3. Write the last scene first and keep it fixed

**Rule.** When the story is long, write the final scene between the central pair before anything else and treat it as fixed. Everything else may be rewritten; that scene may not. A fixed endpoint makes every earlier scene a setup and stops the middle from drifting.

**Naive failure.** The ending is discovered in production and the earlier material is patched to fit. Motifs then fail to pay off, or pay off by accident.

**Example.** Amanda Overton on Arcane: "I think we wrote their last scene together six years ago. And it's never changed. It has stayed our North star, our guiding light for the entire series." Alex Yee: "the story concluding with this season is the one we plotted out from the get-go." Shinichirō Watanabe on Cowboy Bebop: "Even before I made the first episode, I already had the ending in mind," and "all of the episodes that contain Vicious ... are directly related to the ending." DiMartino and Konietzko meant Zuko to end as the hero's teacher before his details were settled.

**Sources.** Overton (Screen Rant red-carpet interview); Yee and Linke (Popverse); Watanabe (Houston interview transcript, Bebop Attic); Avatar planning (Wikipedia's Zuko article, Screen Rant, citing the Braving the Elements podcast).

**Confidence.** High for Arcane and Bebop. Medium for Avatar (secondary).

### P4. A defector must refuse once before they turn

**Rule.** When an enemy will become an ally, give them a scene where they could turn and choose not to. Make the reason a want the audience understands. Place the real turn later, after the cost of the want has been shown. A refused turn makes the eventual turn a decision rather than a plot convenience.

**Naive failure.** The boss is beaten, sees the light and joins up within one scene. Defeat becomes conversion, and the conversion means nothing because it cost nothing.

**Example.** At the end of Avatar's second season, Zuko stands between his uncle, who asks him to choose good, and his sister, who offers what he has always wanted: his honour back and his father's acceptance. He picks his sister and betrays the uncle. Head writer Aaron Ehasz has said the reason was that Zuko "still wasn't ready." The true turn comes several episodes into season 3, after he gets exactly what he wanted and finds it empty. Kirkman on Invincible's Omni-Man: redemption "is a difficult road to travel." Ehasz planned a later Azula redemption "longer and far more complicated than Zuko's": the harder the character, the longer the road.

**Sources.** Wikipedia, "The Crossroads of Destiny" (reports Ehasz); Kirkman (Screen Rant); Ehasz on Azula (Gizmodo, The Mary Sue).

**Confidence.** High for the on-screen sequence. Medium for Ehasz's "wasn't ready" (relayed second-hand).

### P5. Turn the enemy by what they witness, not by argument

**Rule.** When an antagonist changes sides, let a small, unobserved act by the hero do it, one that contradicts what the antagonist was taught, followed by evidence of the hero's effect on others. Witness lets the character convince themselves; debate undermines the turn.

**Naive failure.** The hero gives a speech, the enemy lowers the weapon. The audience hears the theme stated and feels the writer's hand.

**Example.** In Samurai Jack season 5, Ashi was raised by a cult to kill Jack. Its high priestess crushes a ladybug for not being part of the master's order. Trapped with Jack inside a giant creature, Ashi watches him gently free a ladybug, and she drops her weapon. The next episodes show her travelling with him and meeting people he has saved, and only then does her allegiance change. The ladybug comes back in the series' last shot.

**Sources.** Wikipedia, Samurai Jack season 5 (episode synopses).

**Confidence.** High. The sequence can be checked on screen.

### P6. Plant the betrayal in every earlier scene, and change the order so readers are still surprised

**Rule.** When a trusted figure will betray the hero, every earlier scene between them should hold a hint you only see on a rewatch: a hesitation, an evasion, an over-correction. The twist then feels inevitable afterwards and surprising on first viewing. Open and close the arc on large events so the middle has a frame.

**Naive failure.** The traitor is cartoonish from the start (no surprise), or entirely clean until the reveal (no inevitability).

**Example.** Kirkman on Invincible season 1: "Every scene of season one between them, if you go back and watch, hints at what is coming." He also says: "By changing and rearranging things, even if you have read the comics, you still get the sense of surprise." On structure: eight episodes let the season "begin and end ... with these massive, monumental events." The first episode ends with the father's massacre of the hero team, and the last is the father-son fight, which ends on the father's hesitation: "It shows there is a huge conflict within him."

**Sources.** Kirkman (AV Club; CBR); showrunner Simon Racioppa on Kirkman's authorship of the finale (/Film).

**Confidence.** High.

### P7. Write dialogue as a trade, never a lecture

**Rule.** When characters discuss ideas (power, debt, loyalty), each line must be an attempt to get something from the other person or to give them something. If no one in the exchange wants anything, cut it. And end every conversation once its trade is settled.

**Naive failure.** Philosophical speeches where the theme is stated outright. The audience hears the writer thinking.

**Example.** Warren Ellis on Castlevania's long dialogue scenes: "These aren't lectures. These are people talking and trying to get something from each other, or trying to give something to each other." And: "It's not difficult to know when you've outstayed your welcome. All conversations have an arc and a conclusion." In Arcane's first act, Silco's childhood trauma (being held under water) is delivered not as a flashback but as a threat. He tells the story while holding a disloyal subordinate under the river. The backstory is the leverage.

**Sources.** Ellis (Collider); Arcane Act 1 (on screen).

**Confidence.** High for Ellis. High for the Silco scene's content; Medium for which episode of Act 1 it falls in.

### P8. At the emotional peak, take the words out and let picture and music carry it

**Rule.** At a scene's highest emotional pressure, remove dialogue and give it to image, timing and score, because words invite the viewer to evaluate rather than feel. Plan the silence in advance.

**Naive failure.** Characters name their feelings at the peak ("I can't believe you're gone"). Or the music is so loud it tells the viewer what to feel instead of carrying the feeling.

**Example.** Tartakovsky: "There's too much dialogue on animated shows nowadays, and I try to stand out by forcing you to pay attention through silence." And: "Let the music be the voice of the emotion." Primal's Emmy-winning "Plague of Madness" holds a whole tragedy, an infected giant that destroys its own herd and dies in agony, with no dialogue. Arcane director Barthélemy Maunoury on Isha's sacrifice (S2E7): "all the elements that appear in the image are at the service of the narrative, right down to the color ... nothing is left to chance." He drew childlike doodles over a shot, "as if Isha had drawn her memories": memory as image, not speech.

**Sources.** Tartakovsky (search excerpt; AWN); Wikipedia, "Plague of Madness"; Maunoury (Cartoon Brew).

**Confidence.** High for Maunoury and AWN. Medium for the "silence" quote (search excerpt).

### P9. Explain through objects, not lore

**Rule.** When backstory must reach the audience, put it in a physical object the character carries, wears, uses or refuses to use. Let the object's state (damaged, given away, picked up again) report the change. The viewer works out the history, which they enjoy, and the object can later be used as a callback.

**Naive failure.** An opening crawl, a lore dump, or a flashback that halts the present-day story to explain it.

**Example.** In Arcane, Vander's heavy gauntlets are the weapons of a violent past he has set aside to keep the peace; when Vi picks them up, the legacy passes without a line. Cowboy Bebop's Jet Black keeps a cybernetic arm rather than a biological replacement as a "reminder of the consequences of his actions," so his betrayal as a police officer lives in the arm before any episode explains it.

**Sources.** Arcane (on screen); Wikipedia, List of Cowboy Bebop characters (a wiki summary of in-show facts).

**Confidence.** Medium. The Arcane reading is mine, and the claim that the bridge flashback shows the gauntlets being dropped comes from a fan source. The Bebop facts are High.

### P10. Repeat a motif so that its meaning flips

**Rule.** When an image, phrase or object recurs, the second appearance must mean something different, usually the opposite or a completion of the first. If a callback means the same thing twice, it is a wink. If it has changed, it is an arc.

**Naive failure.** Callbacks as fan service: the catchphrase repeated for the cheer, which changes nothing.

**Example.** Samurai Jack season 5 brackets its central relationship with ladybugs. One is crushed by the cult (obedience), one is freed by Jack (mercy), and one is watched at the end among blossoms after a loss (peace with what it cost). In Spider-Verse, the "leap of faith" is set up as Miles' failure to jump and is paid off as a jump the script describes as rising, not falling. Linke warns against the naive version: "the worst thing we can do is just a bunch of fan service."

**Sources.** Wikipedia, Samurai Jack season 5; the Spider-Verse script line, as quoted in a search excerpt; Linke (Screen Rant interview with Linke and Yee).

**Confidence.** High for Samurai Jack. Medium for the Spider-Verse script wording.

### P11. Let structure carry the theme

**Rule.** When a theme matters, encode it in the arrangement of the story (time skips, intercutting, the order of episodes, what is held back) rather than in anyone's mouth. Structure is the one thing an audience cannot skim past.

**Naive failure.** A character speaks the theme ("we're all prisoners of the past"). Or a structure with nothing to do with the theme (a flashback simply because flashbacks are in fashion).

**Example.** Alex Yee on Arcane season 1: "Episodes 1-3 and then 4-9 really were separate shows. 1-3 was the prologue for the back half." The years-long time skip between them is the theme: what was done to the children becomes who the adults are. Avatar's "Zuko Alone" intercuts the exiled prince protecting a village with flashbacks to his family's cruelty; he saves the villagers, tells them his name, and is rejected for it. Inherited identity lands without anyone stating it. Blue Eye Samurai: "We wanted each episode to feel like an episode ... Each episode is a full film."

**Sources.** Yee (AwardsDaily); Wikipedia, "Zuko Alone"; Green and Noizumi (Gizmodo).

**Confidence.** High.

### P12. Charge the ending a real price, but don't confuse misery with depth

**Rule.** When the ending must feel earned, it must cost the protagonist something they valued. That cost must be the direct price of the victory, not an unrelated tragedy. Then grant one small image of what survived.

**Naive failure.** One of two:

- A clean win where everyone lives and the world is fixed (felt as cheating).
- An arbitrary death for gravitas (felt as cruelty).

**Example.** Tartakovsky on the Samurai Jack finale, where victory erases the ally he loves: "I feel like we would be cheating if everything was happy and perfect at the end ... It didn't feel right." Alex Yee on Arcane's finale: "It's not a story where everything just works out ... that cost is something that would be felt and something that needs to be felt." Overton on season 2: "we had everybody making mistakes that were unforgivable, and then asked if they could be forgiven."

**Sources.** Tartakovsky (Wikipedia, Samurai Jack season 5); Yee and Overton (Collider).

**Confidence.** High.

### P13. Stage want against need as an actual choice in a scene

**Rule.** When a character's arc is the gap between what they want (external, stated) and what they need (internal, unseen), build a scene where both are on offer at once, from two different people. Make the character choose wrongly first. A need that is only discussed never becomes drama.

**Naive failure.** A mentor states the need, the hero nods, the arc is over.

**Example.** Zuko in Avatar's second-season finale, as in P4. The scene is built so the want (his father's acceptance, offered by his sister) and the need (choosing his own path, offered by his uncle) sit physically on either side of him. Overton gives a compact version for romance in Arcane: the first kiss "needed ... to be the right thing, but for the wrong reason." The action fulfils the want, and the motive shows the need has not yet been met.

**Sources.** Wikipedia, "The Crossroads of Destiny"; Overton (Collider).

**Confidence.** High.

### P14. Show class as geography and economy, not speeches

**Rule.** When the story is about class, debt or exploitation, express it through space (up and down, clean and poisoned, lit and dark), through what flows between the spaces (money, labour, drugs, parts), and through who can cross. Characters should rarely name the system. They should live inside its prices.

**Naive failure.** Speeches about inequality, or poor people who are noble simply for being poor. Both flatten the people inside the system.

**Example.** Arcane's two cities are split vertically, and the bridge between them is where the decisive confrontations happen. Faye's debt in Cowboy Bebop (medical bills she woke up owing after 54 years in cryosleep, made worse by someone she trusted) is never lectured about; it is a standing pressure on her choices, never cleared. Castlevania's forgemasters are craftsmen whose rare skill makes them valuable to the powerful: Hector's naivety gets him enslaved for his craft, while Isaac turns his into his own agenda.

**Sources.** Fantasy/Animation essay (secondary); Wikipedia, List of Cowboy Bebop characters; Ellis on Castlevania season 3's separate storylines for Isaac and Hector (SyFy Wire).

**Confidence.** Medium overall; the readings are partly mine, but each can be checked on screen.

### P15. Make found family cost something, and let it come apart

**Rule.** When a group forms out of necessity, show what each member gives up to belong and let at least one member leave. A family that can dissolve is a family; one that cannot is a reward screen.

**Naive failure.** The group hug as a prize at the end of the arc, with no member paying anything to stay.

**Example.** Cowboy Bebop's crew is held together by money and a shared ship, not declared love. Near the end Ed and Ein leave, then Spike leaves to settle his past. Watanabe on Spike: "he makes sure he doesn't show it." Arcane's Vander raises children who are not his, and the cost of that choice drives Act 1.

**Sources.** Wikipedia, List of Cowboy Bebop characters; Watanabe (Bebop Attic); Yee on Vander (SyFy Wire): "We always knew Vander would do anything to protect the kids."

**Confidence.** High.

### P16. Ration the reveals, and know what to cut

**Rule.** When a story has many threads, budget one major reveal per unit (episode, region) and cut any thread that cannot be paid off in the available time. Overstuffing is the most commonly reported failure of a prestige series' final season.

**Naive failure.** Every thread kept, every character served, and the finale tries to land all of them at once.

**Example.** Samurai Jack season 5 gave each episode one reveal that turns the show in a new direction (Wikipedia production summary). Arcane season 2 is the cautionary case. Linke: "Some people there, they think it's maybe rushed. There's too much going on," and "It would have been great to have more time ... but we didn't have it." Reportedly the finale's early cut ran over an hour and action was trimmed to fit. Overton: "Let's put 50 pounds of stuff in this bag." Linke contrasts season 1, "focused on more human problems," with a season 2 that "became more conceptual"; the human-scale season is the one critics praised without reservation.

**Sources.** Linke (Inverse; GameRant; GamesRadar); Overton (Collider); Wikipedia, Samurai Jack season 5.

**Confidence.** High for the quotes. Medium for the detail of the hour-long early cut (secondary summary).

### P17. Ration violence so it means something; the decisive fight is an argument

**Rule.** When a story is full of combat, hold back the most intimate violence for the moments that decide a relationship. In those fights every exchange should change the relationship as well as the health bars.

**Naive failure.** The climactic fight is just bigger than the earlier ones. A spectacle boss with more hit points and no new meaning.

**Example.** Tartakovsky on Samurai Jack season 5: "We decided our violence needed to be controlled, and when we do it, it's going to mean something." Kirkman wanted the dialogue of Invincible's father-son finale to be "as brutal" as its violence, and the fight ends on the father's choice not to finish it. In Spider-Verse, Miles unmasks mid-fight and his uncle, recognising him, refuses the kill. Refusing to finish someone is a turn only a fight can stage.

**Sources.** Tartakovsky (IndieWire, via search excerpt); Kirkman (AV Club); film text.

**Confidence.** Medium for the Tartakovsky quote. High for the scenes.

### P18. In short form, start from one image and pay the viewer back before the end

**Rule.** When the form is very short (one card, one short), start from a single image that holds the conflict, build only the minimum situation around it, and give the reward (a turn, a reversal, a cost) inside the piece. Short pieces fail when they act like trailers.

**Naive failure.** A compressed synopsis, or a setup with no payoff that promises a fuller story somewhere else.

**Example.** Alberto Mielgo on Love, Death + Robots' "Jibaro": "The first thing that came to my mind was the visual of a siren singing. And then, I wanted to create a situation around that." On form: "It is almost like a completely different technique," and "with longer shows, we are losing the ability to be precise." Bebop's fifth episode shows the inverse at series scale. Watanabe, knowing viewers decide by episode five, chose to "cram the episode with all the ideas I had for the series," and that episode became the template for the finale.

**Sources.** Mielgo (80.lv; Game Rant summary); Watanabe (/Film).

**Confidence.** High.

## Part 2. Cross-cutting observations

1. **The ending is fixed; the dialogue is not.** Ellis rewrote characters around their actors' performances, Yee says the Silco-Jinx relationship "developed organically" with the voice actor, and Rothman calls the animation script "a living, breathing thing ... For years." For Death Ride, rewrite the cards once the first playable boss exists.
2. **The human scale beats the conceptual scale.** Linke's own contrast of the two Arcane seasons (P16) matches the critical record. The relationships are what people remember. Lore is what they forgive.

## Part 3. Implications for Death Ride

These turn the craft into design decisions. They invent nothing from the sources' plots. Names other than Marrow and "the Mechanic" are placeholders.

### D1. Write the final card first, and lock it (P3)

Before region 1 is scripted, write the last three-line card of the finale: the Mechanic, the improvised rig and Marrow, at the moment the fight resolves. Freeze it. Every other card, boss and motif is checked against it: does this set up that? This is the cheapest insurance against a campaign that drifts across five regions.

### D2. Treat region 1 as the prologue, which is a different game in tone (P11)

Region 1 should make the debt feel survivable, even fun: small purses, a lenient Marrow, a car the player loves. Regions 2 to 5 take apart what it built, so the structure itself says how a debt grows. Mark the gap with one beat the player notices on the ledger screen, such as a changed rate.

### D3. Give Marrow a legitimate grievance and an illegitimate method (P2)

Marrow should be right about something the player benefits from. Suggestion: the league is the only thing keeping fuel, parts and medicine moving through the wasteland, and the debt system pays for it. His method (debt bondage, seizure, staged deaths on the track) makes him the villain. The player's victory must then answer "what runs the roads after Marrow?", even if in one card.

### D4. Give Marrow one protected thing, show it without words, and make the finale charge for it (P1)

Marrow needs one bond he would trade the league for. Show it in two or three wordless moments across the campaign, in the background of a card or in an object in his garage. Do not reveal its history in dialogue. In the finale, the fight's outcome should hinge on his refusing to give it up. Do not copy the surrogate-child structure. Options that fit a racer:

- a car he has never raced;
- a driver he lost and still keeps a grid slot for;
- the league's founding track, which he will not let be paved over.

### D5. Make the car seizure the theme in physical form, and make the player fight their own car (P9, P10)

The seizure is the emotional low point and can be the game's best motif. Suggestion: in the finale Marrow drives the seized car, carrying every upgrade the player bought on credit. The player faces their own investment turned against them: debt as an object. In hindsight, the upgrade menu was the ledger.

### D6. Each boss refuses once before turning (P4)

A boss beaten in their own region must not join the player straight away. Their defeat card should show them choosing Marrow again, for a want the player understands: a family member's debt, a title, protection for their pit crew. The turn comes later, in a following region, and costs the boss something visible with Marrow (a lost garage, a revoked licence, an injury). Two refusal lengths are enough: short for the early bosses, long for the boss whose turn matters most.

### D7. Bosses turn because of what they witness the player do, not because of a speech (P5)

Script each turn as the boss seeing the player do something small and costly that contradicts what Marrow taught them, such as hauling a wrecked rival out of a burning ring or refusing a purse that came from a seizure. Best of all, make it a real player choice from an earlier race, replayed on the boss's card, so the turn rests on the player's own conduct.

### D8. Turned bosses pay in parts, so the rig is the found family (P15, P10)

Build the Mechanic's mine-laying rig from components each turned boss gives up from their own car: a gearbox, armour plating, a mine rack, a fuel cell. The found family is then physical and on screen in every frame of the finale, each turn has a cost (the boss is weaker on the track), and the rig recalls five regions without a line of recap.

### D9. The young Mechanic: want against need, a skill used by others, and a scene where the want is offered (P13, P14)

- **Want (stated):** to be left alone to fix things and clear a debt of their own to Marrow, which ties them to the theme.
- **Need (unseen):** to build something that is theirs rather than servicing other people's machines.
- **The choice scene:** Marrow offers to cancel the Mechanic's debt in exchange for the rig, or for tuning the seized car. Let them hesitate, perhaps fail once. Nervousness shows in the craft (over-checking bolts, apologising to the machine), never in a line about it.
- **Plant:** the Mechanic once serviced Marrow's fleet and knows the seized car's weak points. Set it up in region 1; reveal it in region 4 or 5.

### D10. The final fight is an argument in the shape of a fight, and it changes rules mid-fight (P17, P6)

The finale is to the death, so every phase change should stand for a change in the relationship, not just more health:

- **Phase 1:** Marrow plays it as a collection, toying with the player.
- **Phase 2:** the rig's mines (the Mechanic's craft) hurt him, and he takes it personally.
- **Phase 3:** he would rather wreck the car (and himself) than lose it to the person who owes him. The death is his refusal to be owed.

Plant the killing blow in region 1: a track feature, a league rule, or the mine the Mechanic was too nervous to arm in a tutorial. Allow Marrow one line per phase at most, and let the score carry the last.

### D11. Story cards are three lines, and each line has a job (P18, P8, P7)

Rules for the card format:

- **Line 1, the image:** a place or object, concrete, readable at TV distance. No names unless the name is the point.
- **Line 2, the trade:** what someone wants from someone else, in the present. Not backstory.
- **Line 3, the turn or the price:** the thing that is now different. Never a feeling named outright.
- **Ban list:** the words "feel", "realise", "remember", "destiny" and "betrayal". No line explains a line above it.
- **Picture first:** the card's art carries the scene; the text is a caption. On a TV the image is the main thing read, and the prose only labels it.

Illustrative cards (original, for shape only):

> The ledger board lights a new row under your plate number.\
> Marrow's clerk wants the title deed before the starting flag.\
> You sign; the engine sounds the same, and isn't yours.

> Her pit crew is three kids and a cutting torch.\
> She takes your offer of the spare tyres and says nothing.\
> Next race, her car carries your number painted under hers.

### D12. Ration reveals: one per region, and cut threads you cannot pay (P16)

With five regions, allow five major reveals and no more. A workable spread:

1. Marrow's grievance.
2. A boss's hidden debt.
3. The Mechanic's history with the fleet.
4. Marrow's protected thing.
5. The cost of the seizure.

If a sixth thread appears in writing (a rival romance, a second villain, the league's founder), cut it or fold it into one of the five. Arcane season 2 is the documented cost of keeping everything.

### D13. Show class as track geography and the flow of money (P14)

Upper tiers of a circuit are paved and sponsored; lower loops run through scrapyards of seized cars, where the player's own car can later be glimpsed. On the results screen, show a fraction of the purse going to the player and the rest climbing to Marrow's ledger. No character explains the system.

### D14. Plant Marrow's endgame in every card he appears in (P6)

Every earlier card with Marrow should hold one detail that only reads properly after the seizure. Examples:

- He compliments the car, not the driver.
- He has the player's chassis number memorised.
- His garage has an empty bay with the player's plate number already stencilled on the floor.

Seen the first time, he is generous. In hindsight he is foreclosing.

### D15. The ending's price: what the player keeps (P12)

The victory should cost the player what they started racing for: the seized car does not survive the finale. Grant one surviving image: the rough rig, built by a nervous kid from five defectors' parts, still running. No sequel hook that undoes the cost, and no off-screen death of a turned boss for weight.

## Part 4. Confidence and gaps

Per-claim confidence sits under each pattern. In short:

- **Strongest (High):** the fixed endings (Overton, Watanabe), Act 1 as prologue (Yee), the planted betrayal (Kirkman), dialogue as trade (Ellis), witness over argument (Samurai Jack).
- **Weakest (Medium or Low):** Ehasz's "wasn't ready" (relayed second-hand), the Spider-Verse script wording (search excerpt only), and the Arcane gauntlet and class-geography readings (partly my own).
- **D1 to D15** are design proposals inferred from the patterns, not facts.
