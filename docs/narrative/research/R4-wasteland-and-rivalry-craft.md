# R4: Wasteland, rivalry and debt, a craft dossier for Death Ride

Research slice R4, 2026-10-04. Scope: storytelling craft in wasteland, vehicular and company-town settings, and the owner's themes: survival at stake, debt and control, found allies. This is research and options, not an owner decision. It reads existing canon in `docs/concepts/deathride/Q0-ash-circuit-plot.md`, `G0-region-bible.md` and `K1-campaign-directions.md` (Rook/Scrap, Ox/Foundry, Vex/Salt and quarry, Mica/Switchback, Marrow/Crown, the Mechanic, Relay, the missing sibling, the ledger, the seizure) and changes none of it.

**Format.** Each pattern: a rule (when X, do Y, because Z), the naive failure it prevents, an example that can be checked, sources, and a confidence note. **High** = a primary source (creator interview, the work, a primary text) states it. **Medium** = credible secondary analysis or a creator statement reported by a journalist. **Low** = our inference. Works are studied for craft only; nothing proposes reusing a plot, a character or a line.

---

## 1. A believable world without explanation

**P1. Make the economy the map.**
*Rule:* when a world must be legible in a few frames, define it by two or three goods everyone needs and who controls each, because an economy explains politics, geography and motive at once. *Naive failure:* regions defined by colour and weather; nobody knows why anyone lives there or fights. *Example:* Fury Road's world is a triangle of water, fuel and ammunition, each held by one stronghold; the chase starts because a convoy meant to carry goods carries people. *Sources:* Cinephilia & Beyond; Wikipedia, Fury Road. *Confidence:* high (on screen).

**P2. Write the deep backstory; show only its residue.**
*Rule:* when dialogue is scarce (a three-line card is scarcer than any film), write private biographies for every character, car and place, then show only their marks: a painted door, a ritual phrase, a scar, because players read consistency as depth. *Naive failure:* no backstory (characters as skins) or all of it dumped on a loading screen. *Example:* George Miller: "we sat down and wrote deep backstories, really exploring this world in very fine detail"; the film was storyboarded in about 3,500 panels before dialogue was added, and "people only speak when it's necessary." A War Rig door painted with a skeletal arm where the driver's arm is missing tells a history without a word. *Sources:* Den of Geek interview; Cinephilia & Beyond (Total Film quote); Wikipedia. *Confidence:* high.

**P3. Politics in the background, the person in the foreground.**
*Rule:* when the subject is structural (debt, class, a league that owns people), keep the protagonist's personal want in front and let the system show behind every scene, because a system stared at directly becomes a lecture. *Naive failure:* the villain explains the oppression. *Example:* Žižek on Children of Men: "the true focus of the film is there in the background"; look at oppression "too directly" and "you don't see it." Cages and propaganda pass behind a man trying to get one person to the coast. *Sources:* Žižek DVD commentary (bavatuesdays transcript); Wolmart, International Journal of Žižek Studies; Senses of Cinema. *Confidence:* high.

**P4. A used world is repaired, not ruined.**
*Rule:* show repairs and retrofits, not only damage, because accretion implies people who stayed and kept things running; ruin implies only a past. *Naive failure:* identical rust and rubble in every region. *Example:* Ridley Scott wanted Blade Runner's things to look as if they "had to be jacked up a little bit to work at all"; Syd Mead layered repairs over clean designs. Josh Sawyer describes Fallout communities that have "moved beyond simply living in the ruins". *Sources:* Boing Boing interview with Scott; American Cinematographer; Sawyer on post-apocalyptic games. *Confidence:* high.

**P5. Leave the catastrophe unexplained; explain the rules of living.**
*Rule:* spend words on how people live now, not how the world ended, because survivors care about consequences. *Naive failure:* a lore crawl before the first race. *Example:* The Road never names its catastrophe and never defines its central code ("carrying the fire"); the reader assembles both. *Sources:* The Quietus; Church Life Journal. *Confidence:* high.

## 2. Debt as a leash

**P6. Debt makes violence look like the victim's fault.**
*Rule:* when the antagonist controls through debt, let him speak only of obligation and fairness, never of force, because debt's power is making the debtor feel he is in the wrong. *Naive failure:* the creditor is a thug; the player feels anger but no shame, so the leash is a mere obstacle. *Example:* Graeber: there is "no better way to justify relations founded on violence... than by reframing them in the language of debt", which "makes it seem that it's the victim who's doing something wrong"; what makes a debt is that the creditor can "specify, numerically, exactly how much the debtor owes." *Sources:* Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*; Naked Capitalism interview. *Confidence:* high on the text; application to games is inference.

**P7. A leash is a debt the creditor can rewrite, paid in his currency.**
*Rule:* let the creditor control the unit of account, the shop, repair prices and the terms, because a debt you can pay off is a loan; one whose terms change is a leash. *Naive failure:* a fixed number that falls predictably; once the player does the arithmetic the threat is gone. *Example:* Appalachian coal companies paid in scrip spendable only at the company store, advanced wages in scrip at 50 to 80 percent of value, and many miners never cleared the store debt; "Sixteen Tons" made the image folk memory. Economic historians dispute how universal the gouging was; the institution felt soul-collecting regardless. *Sources:* Wikipedia, Company scrip and Company store; e-WV, Scrip; Fishback, *Journal of Economic History*. *Confidence:* high on history, medium on generality.

**P8. Show the leash bite someone else first, and make leaving worse than staying.**
*Rule:* before the debt bites the player, show it bite a character the player knows, and let the controller point out that the player is free to go, because a witnessed punishment needs no explanation and chosen captivity is more frightening than a cage. *Naive failure:* the seizure is a surprise, or a rule says "you cannot leave", which reads as structure, not menace. *Example:* Squid Game lets indebted players vote to leave and shows most of them come back; Hwang Dong-hyuk: "once you fail at the competition, then you cannot ever recover." Death Rally's loan shark takes an upgrade if unpaid after three races. Death Race (2008) has a private prison sell pay-per-view races in which inmates are "offered" freedom. *Sources:* UPI and Tribune reports of Hwang; Wikipedia and TV Tropes, Death Rally; Wikipedia, Death Race (2008). *Confidence:* high on the works; the generalisation is ours (medium).

## 3. A villain who wants control, human and frightening

**P9. The controller is calm, reasonable and partly right.**
*Rule:* give the villain a coherent case for order the player cannot fully refute, said quietly, because a rational tyrant frightens where a raving one amuses. *Naive failure:* a sneering boss whose evil is self-evident. *Example:* Mr. House presents himself as a benefactor; Sawyer calls him "only interested in maintaining independent control, no matter what the cost", and writer John Gonzalez says his view of him changed watching Silicon Valley "would-be messiahs". Sawyer's wider rule: no "clear good guys". *Sources:* Sawyer statements (Fallout wiki archive); Yahoo/PC Gamer on Gonzalez. *Confidence:* high.

**P10. Control is rationing, made visible by architecture.**
*Rule:* show the villain rationing a necessity from a place people must look up to, because chosen scarcity is control you can see. *Naive failure:* power asserted through henchmen counts. *Example:* Fury Road's warlord releases water in seconds-long torrents from a clifftop and warns against becoming "addicted" to it. *Sources:* Shmoop analysis; Hyperreal Film Club. *Confidence:* high on screen; medium on the architectural reading.

**P11. One genuine attachment, expressed as possession.**
*Rule:* give the villain one real attachment that he expresses through dependency and rules, because it makes him pitiable and more frightening at once. *Naive failure:* a tacked-on sad backstory, or a final redemption that dissolves the threat. *Example:* Arcane's Silco gives up his dream of an independent Zaun rather than hand over the girl he raised, yet the same love isolates her; analyses stress it "neither redeems him nor makes him more empathetic" to others. Disco Elysium's union boss Evrart Claire shows warmth and favours as a surveillance network; Helen Hindpere notes "people in Left-wing organizations who sometimes have questionable morals". *Sources:* Wikipedia, Arcane; Medium analysis of Silco; Platypus Review interview with Hindpere. *Confidence:* medium-high.

**P12. Keep the villain present between fights.**
*Rule:* when the antagonist races only at the end, keep him present through a channel (radio, signature, announcer), because a villain met only at the finale is a stranger. *Naive failure:* he appears in the opening card and the final fight only. *Example:* Anthony Burch wanted Borderlands 2's villain to be someone players want to shoot yet still want "to hear what he'll say next", a man "who had a lot of fun being a bad guy", talking to the player throughout over comms; a personal loss later made his lines "darker and angrier", giving him an arc. *Sources:* Sound & Vision and Shacknews interviews (read via summaries; one page refused fetch). *Confidence:* medium-high.

## 4. Rivals into allies, not cheaply

**P13. Respect is earned through mirrored competence, in the activity.**
*Rule:* before a rival joins, show both drivers excellent in opposite ways and let each see the other's excellence in a race, because rivals trust only respect earned on the track. *Naive failure:* a jerk until beaten, then a friend; the turn rewards victory, not relationship. *Example:* Peter Morgan's Rush contrasts Lauda's calculation with Hunt's recklessness and refuses "clear-cut heroes and villains"; the closing voice-over names Hunt among the very few rivals Lauda liked and fewer he respected. The film exaggerates the real enmity for this payoff. *Sources:* Variety review; Wikipedia, Rush. *Confidence:* high.

**P14. The turn must cost the rival what they wanted, and may relapse once.**
*Rule:* make the switching rival give up what they fought for and stumble before it sticks, because a turn without sacrifice reads as the writer moving a piece. *Naive failure:* "you beat me fair and square, I'm with you now." *Example:* Zuko in Avatar: The Last Airbender throws away the honour and approval he chased for two seasons, betrays once more, then repairs damage with actions. *Sources:* Flipscreened, "Writing the redemption arc"; Golden May Editing. *Confidence:* medium (secondary, strong consensus).

**P15. The convert keeps his culture.**
*Rule:* let a former enemy keep his vocabulary and rituals, turned toward the new side, because a convert who drops his identity stops being a person. *Naive failure:* the ex-rival instantly talks like the hero's crew. *Example:* Fury Road's young fanatic still uses his cult's ritual cry at his last act, now addressed to someone on the new side; critics note he "has not abandoned all of his culture". *Sources:* Screen Rant on Nux; University of Plymouth WRASSE. *Confidence:* medium-high.

**P16. Show the rival's side before the turn.**
*Rule:* show a slice of the rival's life away from racing (crew, family, debt, fear) before asking the player to like them, because sympathy borrowed afterwards feels like a retcon. *Example:* Cobra Kai retells an old rivalry from the former antagonist's down-and-out perspective; its creators wanted viewers "really invested in his story", and said the rivals' final reconciliation "was always the idea". *Sources:* Final Draft interview; CBR. *Confidence:* high.

## 5. Found family in a hostile world

**P17. Design the finale's need first, then recruit for it.**
*Rule:* when allies matter to the ending, decide what the ending needs from each ally, then give each an acquisition beat and a trust beat proving they can supply it, because the finale then pays off every relationship and preparations visibly matter. *Naive failure:* allies collected as trophies, irrelevant to the last fight. *Example:* Mass Effect 2 was "built around the suicide mission, as opposed to the other way around"; writer Brian Kindregan: "recruit your team... try to make them loyal, and then go on the suicide mission and see who survives." Disloyal or misassigned companions die first. *Sources:* TheGamer (Kindregan); Wikipedia, Suicide Mission. *Confidence:* high.

**P18. Family is chosen at a cost, inside a shared code or ritual.**
*Rule:* show each member making a costly choice for the others, and give the group a shared rule or ritual, because family by proximity is not family. *Example:* craft commentary: "found family is earned", and post-apocalyptic settings suit it because characters are compelled to depend on each other. Tony Gilroy built Andor's Ferrix through civic institutions and ritual; "the funeral came alive to me through that civic procession", and that ritual makes the town's revolt credible. *Sources:* Writers in the Storm; Sharon Wray; StarWars.com Gilroy interview. *Confidence:* high on Gilroy, medium on the trope claims.

## 6. Betrayal versus loyalty

**P19. Corrode loyalty in witnessed steps.**
*Rule:* stage erosion as acts the loyal character can no longer excuse, with someone whispering the other side, because sudden betrayal is a twist and gradual corrosion is tragedy. *Example:* Red Dead Redemption 2 erodes a gang leader's bond with his surrogate son over chapters; each disproportionate act is seen, doubters are branded disloyal, the leader turns to his one flatterer. *Sources:* Wikipedia, Arthur Morgan; Sidequest essay. *Confidence:* medium-high.

**P20. Let the institution betray; let the friend be tested.**
*Rule:* source betrayal in the system (league, sponsor, paperwork) and make it test a friend, because the audience keeps loving the people while hating the machine. *Naive failure:* a friend is secretly a traitor, poisoning every relationship retroactively. *Example:* in Ford v Ferrari the corporation orders the leading driver to slow for a staged photo finish, then denies him the win on a technicality; the loyalty test falls on the friend who must pass the order on. Twisted Metal's host grants the winner's wish literally; David Jaffe: "you get exactly what you ask for." *Sources:* Go Into The Story analysis; TheWrap fact check; Wikipedia, Twisted Metal; DualShockers interview with Jaffe. *Confidence:* high.

## 7. A fight to the death that feels earned

**P21. The last fight is the last conversation.**
*Rule:* make the duel the culmination of a relationship felt throughout, and leave the final act in the player's hand, because a fight weighs what was said before it. *Naive failure:* a stronger stranger at the top of a ladder. *Example:* Metal Gear Solid 3 ends in a duel with the protagonist's mentor; afterwards the game hands the player the last shot and waits. Critics call it the series' biggest "player punch". *Sources:* Goomba Stomp essay; Game Rant. *Confidence:* high.

**P22. The champion is a threat to the system, so the finale is public.**
*Rule:* when sport is a control tool, a popular winner is a political threat and the finale is the system's attempt to remove him publicly, because then the duel matters beyond two drivers. *Example:* Rollerball (1975): a corporate sport meant to show the futility of individual effort; the star's popularity is the threat, so the rules change until the game is meant to kill him. *Sources:* Wikipedia, Rollerball; The Conversation. *Confidence:* high.

## 8. Regional culture, not five palettes

**P23. Climate makes economy, economy makes custom, custom makes slang.**
*Rule:* derive each region's work from its environment, then customs from the work, then slang from customs, because culture grown from material conditions feels inevitable, while culture assigned by mood board feels like costume. *Naive failure:* five reskins with the same people in different coats. *Example:* Dune derives Fremen ritual from water scarcity, down to taking the water of the dead. Hindpere wanted Disco Elysium's players "to arrive at political conclusions through playing", writing politics through material conditions; Kurvitz refused fantasy's "fig leaf". *Sources:* LitCharts on Dune; Skidmore, "Water discipline"; Platypus Review; PCGamesN interview with Kurvitz. *Confidence:* high.

**P24. Each region contains multitudes, and an institution.**
*Rule:* give every region at least two voices who disagree about its own code, plus one civic institution and one memory object, because monolithic factions paint "too simple of a picture" and institutions generate story. *Example:* Sawyer: New Vegas factions feel real because members "disagree and challenge each other... it contains multitudes." Gilroy's Ferrix has buyers, pickers, a guild hall, a time-keeper and gloves on a wall that raise questions of status and family. *Sources:* PC Gamer interview with Sawyer; StarWars.com; Wikipedia, Rix Road. *Confidence:* high.

## 9. Humour, warmth, and framing violence

**P25. Humour from character under pressure, never the world winking.**
*Rule:* put jokes in specific people's coping (gallows humour, petty grudges, pride) while stakes stay real, because jokes that deny stakes deflate them. *Example:* Cobra Kai's creators: "part of the comedy is the fact that you cry." Hindpere describes processing political defeat through dark jokes. Sawyer traces Fallout's humour to one consistent source, the naive optimism of its pre-war era: a voice, not random gags. *Sources:* Final Draft; Platypus Review; Sawyer statements. *Confidence:* medium-high.

**P26. Satire needs a target; gore without one is noise.**
*Rule:* decide who the violence's joke is on (audience, promoter, rules) and keep it there, because unfocused violence reads as cruelty or as nothing. *Example:* Death Race 2000's director complained that much of his comedy was cut for "crushed heads and blood squibs", yet the broadcast framing kept it a media satire. Carmageddon's censored releases turned pedestrians into zombies or robots: what you hit decides how the act reads. Road Rash framed brutality as an illegal street race with police as enforcers. *Sources:* Wikipedia, Death Race 2000; Movie Malpractice; Wikipedia, Carmageddon; Team VVV; Wikipedia, Road Rash. *Confidence:* high on facts; generalisation ours.

## 10. Political economy of a blood-sport league

**P27. Somebody pays for the games to buy something else.**
*Rule:* decide who funds the league, what they buy with it (votes, legitimacy, fear, labour discipline) and who pays the real price, because spectacle is the means, not the product. *Example:* Roman munera began as funeral duties; sponsors spent ruinous sums for popularity that "paid off in votes", and emperors later monopolised the games because they were power. Suzanne Collins built The Hunger Games from Roman games and channel-surfing between reality TV and war coverage, around "the voyeuristic thrill". Squid Game's VIPs pay and bet. *Sources:* Archaeology Magazine; Futrell, *The Politics of the Arena*; School Library Journal interview with Collins. *Confidence:* high.

| Role | What they get | What they pay |
|---|---|---|
| Owner or promoter | Control, legitimacy, a channel for regional anger, fleet and contract income | Must keep the spectacle fresh and stars dependent |
| Regional bosses | Standing, a cut of contracts, a licence to keep their people working | Their best drivers, their autonomy, part of every purse |
| Drivers | A chance to clear debt or win fame | Cars, bodies, often lives |
| Audience | Catharsis, local pride, betting, watching someone else pay | Attention and money; they are the system's real constituency |
| The excluded | Nothing, or bread | The salvage, labour and bodies the league turns into fleet |

**P28. Endings land when they return, pay and leave one image.**
*Rule:* end where the journey began, changed by the people gathered on the way, with a visible cost and one concrete image of the new order. *Examples:* Rush ends on respect, not a trophy; Cobra Kai on a planned reconciliation; The Road on bounded hope; Twisted Metal on a wish granted as written; Rollerball on a crowd chanting a name. *Confidence:* medium (synthesis).

## 11. Implications and options for Death Ride

Items 1 to 8 deepen the current frame. Items 9 to 14 are **frame changes** with what, why, cost and risks. None is an owner decision.

**1. Marrow speaks only fairness (P6, P9).** Every Marrow card and receipt uses words like settle, honour, owed, account; never threats. Q0's ledger already separates payment, credit, diversion and interest; add one Marrow line per receipt type. Cost: text. Risk: low.

**2. The unit of account is his (P7).** Keep CR, but show league payments converted into "Crown marks" at a rate he quotes. Ox's receipts then expose the conversion as well as the diversion. Marks are never spendable, so there is no second currency. Cost: labels and one ledger column. Risk: confusion; test the receipt screen.

**3. The leash bites someone else in Act 1 (P8).** K1 says Rook "fears his own creditor". Show that creditor post a lien on a car Rook minds, so the Crown seizure later is a promise kept, not a twist. Cost: one or two cards and a lien-tag prop variant. Risk: none to rules.

**4. Marrow's one attachment is the Crown (P11, P9).** He maintains the timing gear himself while the stands rot, and believes the league alone stops five regions from killing each other; he is partly right. This humanises him without redemption and gives the finale a symbol. Cost: bio plus a few cards. Risk: too much sympathy; keep K1's "people as depreciating equipment" voice.

**5. Keep Marrow present (P12).** One Marrow line on the league PA before each boss race and his signature on every receipt. His habit can be correcting other people's arithmetic. Cost: five to ten lines for the voice run. Risk: repetition; vary by act.

**6. Three earned beats per rival (P13 to P16).** Within each act's seven cards: (a) the rival under the leash, away from racing; (b) a race where the existing signature weaknesses (K1: Rook punishes late lines, Ox overcommits on tight corners, Vex needs straights, Mica rewards surface reading) become mirrored competence; (c) one line on what joining costs them. Cost: card authoring. Risk: none mechanical.

**7. Allies keep codes and grudges (P15).** Q0 lets a collision grudge coexist with loyalty and forbids invisible boosts. Lean in: allies taunt in their own slang (table below) and may refuse a contract that breaks their code. Cost: taunt sets. Risk: low.

**8. The Mechanic's secrecy as a fair suspicion that resolves (P19, P20).** Q0 fixes `mechanicLoyal=1` and secrecy as fear. Within that, plant two clues that could read as betrayal (absences, vanishing parts), then reveal that Marrow offered to clear the debt for the sibling's location and the Mechanic refused. The institution supplies the pressure; the friend passes the test. Cost: three cards. Risk: lingering distrust; resolve before Act 5. Add one warm habit (counting bolts aloud, apologising to the car) as the recurring comic note (P25).

**9. Frame change: allies have jobs in the final fight (P17).** *What:* Q0's duel stays one-on-one (the Mechanic's rig versus Marrow's Bulwark). Each recruited ally gets one visible, named contribution announced before it: Ox's crew rebuilt the rig's seams, Vex supplied the timing of the Crown's pickups, Mica's people cut the floodlights on a signal, Rook brought the lien papers that make the fight public. *Why:* the finale becomes the sum of the campaign; found family is felt in the fight, not only the epilogue. *Cost:* S as cards only; M if any contribution changes the arena, which then needs disclosed rules and fairness gates. *Risks:* diluting the Mechanic's moment (allies prepare, the Mechanic drives); breaking "no invisible boost" (presentation only, or a disclosed rule).

**10. Frame change: Marrow on track once before the finale (P21, P12).** *What:* a non-competitive appearance, such as a lap of honour in the Bulwark before an Act 3 or Act 4 boss race, or a pace car the field may not pass. *Why:* the player has felt his mass and line before needing to wreck it; the duel becomes a reckoning, not a first meeting. *Cost:* M (parade or pace AI, event flag, tests). *Risks:* players will try to hit him; an untouchable car can feel like a cheat. Cheaper alternative: his car on the grid in a card backdrop.

**11. Frame change: open at the Crown (P28).** *What:* one opening card at the Crown, under floodlights and a full crowd, where the debt is signed. The finale returns to the same place, stands empty. *Why:* the ending becomes a return; the Crown is a known place, not just a final level. *Cost:* one card reusing the Crown backdrop. *Risk:* front-loaded exposition (P5); one image, three lines.

**12. Frame change: the regions fill the stands (P22, P27, P28).** *What:* G0 calls the Crown a "hollow spectacle" with empty grandstands. Make that plot: the league is losing its audience as the regions go broke, and the seizure and death duel are Marrow's bid for a public example. The allies bring their towns; the epilogue's stands are full, on the player's side. *Why:* it answers who watches and why, makes the duel political, and gives an ending image earned by all four allies. *Cost:* S as cards; M if crowd art is wanted (G0 forbids faces, so silhouettes and pennants). *Risks:* art budget; the crowd must never seem to affect physics.

**13. Keep the region order and say why (P1, P23).** Scrap, Foundry, Salt and quarry, Switchback, Crown follows the league's own supply chain: salvage sorted, melted into fleet, hauled across the basin, moved over the only pass, spent at the speedway. The player follows the money upstream to its owner, and the climb into thin air before the descent to the Crown is a natural crescendo. Swapping Salt and Switchback would reach the sibling earlier but break both the supply-chain logic and the altitude arc. **Recommendation: do not reorder.** Cost: zero.

**14. Frame change: the restored car keeps one scar (P28, P14).** *What:* Q0 restores the seized car and voids the balance; keep both, but return the car with one permanent mark (a lien tag welded to the dash) that the player can keep or remove at the garage. *Why:* an ending lands when something was paid; this costs no progression. *Cost:* S as a card and garage flag. *Risk:* clashing with car art rules; keep it in garage UI, not on the race sprite.

## 14. Confidence and limits

- **High:** world through economy and residue (P1, P2), reasonable controllers (P9), finale-first ally design (P17), culture from material conditions and internal dissent (P23, P24), the political economy of spectacle (P27). Each rests on primary statements or texts.
- **Medium:** redemption craft (P14) and found family (P18) lean on secondary criticism with broad consensus. Burch, Sasko and Collins were read through journalist summaries (one page refused fetch, one was truncated); quotes are as reported.
- **Ours (low to medium):** "show the leash on someone else first" (P8) as a general rule, the ending synthesis (P28), and every option and table entry in sections 11 and 12.
- **Gaps:** Death Rally, Road Rash and Carmageddon via public descriptions only, as briefed. Cyberpunk 2077 is represented by its quest director's process statements, not quest-level analysis.
